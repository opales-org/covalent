Covalent Supervisor

See a running Covalent application from the inside: its components, how they are wired, what they exchange, and how they move through their lifecycle.

The supervisor observes one managed container at a time. Its window is organized around the rail on the left:

  • Cockpit: live figures of the container, and a console to drive its components' lifecycle.
  • Components: the component diagram, with live activity on its wires.
  • Sequences: recordings of the calls and events exchanged between components.
  • Graph: the dependency graph and its cycles.
  • Threads: shows what the worker pools and their threads are doing.
  • Exclusions: the components the supervisor leaves out.
  • Stereotypes: the stereotype shown for each component, from the end of its name. It has no button on the rail: it is a tab of Settings.
  • Settings: preferences and colors.
  • Help: this help, the keyboard shortcuts, and the About box.

Each function can show several tabs at once, for instance two component diagrams side by side in tabs. The header holds the container selector, the Flows switch, the Record button and the light and dark theme switch. The status bar shows where you are and the zoom of the visible diagram: drag the slider or use its − and + buttons, which go by steps, to zoom around the middle of the view (or the selected component); double-click the percentage to go back to 100 %. The mouse wheel zooms around the pointer.

Observing a container

Pick the container to observe in the selector at the top of the window, then click Connect. The selector lists the managed containers of the application. Only managed containers can be observed.

The supervisor follows the observed container across its lifecycle. When the container is stopping or being disposed, the supervisor disconnects, empties its views and shows the container as Stopped or Disposed. When the container is started again, or a new container is created under the same name, it connects again on its own.

Data flows

The Flows switch turns on the observation of the calls, events and parameter reads exchanged in the container: the live activity on the diagram wires, the rates of the cockpit and the recordings all come from these flows. They cost a little on the observed application, so they are off unless you turn them on, or ask for them in Settings when connecting. Turning the flows off stops a recording in progress.

Cockpit

The cockpit summarizes the observed container. It has two tabs: Overview and Console. The badge next to its title tells whether the supervisor is connected.

Overview

  • Components: how many components the container holds, managed and raw, and how many are composites.
  • Wiring: the links between components, by kind: services, events and parameters.
  • Service calls and Event notifications: current rates, with their recent trend.
  • Failures: the failures over the last five minutes.
  • Throughput: messages per second over the chosen window (15 s, 1 min, 5 min or 15 min), per kind of message.
  • Lifecycle: how many components are in each lifecycle state, and the recent state transitions.
  • Top-level composites: one card per composite, with the state of its components and its message rate. Open a card to see the composite in the component diagram.
  • Threads: what the worker pools are doing: the share of busy workers over the last minute while task tracking is on, the threads otherwise, and saturated pools, rejected tasks or a deadlock. Click it to open Threads.
  • Hottest ports: the busiest ports, with their owner, rate and 95th percentile duration.

Rates need the data flows: with flows off, they stay at zero.

Console

The console drives the lifecycle of the observed components: activate, passivate, restart, remove, undeploy and so on, component by component or for the whole container (Activate all, Passivate all, Remove all). Only the commands that make sense in a component's current state are offered. A component whose transition failed shows the failed state, and only Reset (or Reset all) is offered for it: reset cleans up its composite and brings it back to deployed, without starting it.

Each slice is a composite and shows its direct children. A child composite is shown as a channel with one dot per component it holds, a switch that applies to all of them, and › to enter it. A slice holding composites stands out as a stack of cards: click it to enter it, and its children become the slices. The breadcrumb above the slices, or Up one level, goes back up. The Standalone slice gathers the components outside any composite of the level shown. The container panel at the bottom is the same at every level.

These commands act on a running application, so the console starts disarmed: click Arm to show the container's commands. A line shows the time left: they disarm on their own after 60 seconds, or at once with Disarm. Commands with lasting effects, such as removing or undeploying, ask for confirmation. Covalent's own internal components are shown but cannot be driven: the container manages them.

Components

The component diagram shows the components of the observed container as cards, and the links between their ports. New diagram opens another diagram in a new tab.

Reading the diagram

  • Each card shows a component, its lifecycle state badge, and its ports around its edges.
  • Ports and links are colored by kind: services, events and parameters. A provided service, a produced event or an offered parameter is drawn differently from a used service, a consumed event or an accessed parameter. The port color legend button lists them.
  • A composite holds other components. Double-click it, or use Enter composite in its menu, to see its inside. The breadcrumb above the diagram shows the current level: click a level to go back up, or use Up one level.
  • A port whose link leaves the level shown carries a small navigation badge: it jumps straight to the component at the other end, without losing the current drill-down.
  • With data flows on, the wires light up as calls and events go through them, and turn to the failure color when a call fails.

The component tree and the details panel

The tree on the left lists every component by composite, with a search field. Selecting a component, in the tree or in the diagram, shows its details on the right: its state and its ports, with buttons to show its dependencies, hide it, or record a sequence of its activity. Both side panels can be folded.

Selecting and moving

  • Click a card to select it, Ctrl+click to add it to the selection or remove it.
  • Shift+drag selects the cards in an area, Ctrl+drag adds them to the selection.
  • Drag a card to move it, drag the background to pan, and use the mouse wheel to zoom.
  • Right-click for a menu. A card: hide it, show or hide its dependencies, enter a composite. A link: highlight it, go to its source or its target, record a sequence between the two components, hide it. A port: highlight, go to a component linked to it. The background: show all components, recenter.

The selection drives the Record button of the header: see Sequences.

Views library

A component diagram view (the level shown, the hidden components, the positions, the selection and the zoom) can be kept in the views library.

  • Save view… (Ctrl+S) names the view, with an optional description, for the observed container. A name is unique for its container: saving under an existing name replaces that view after a warning. In a tab opened from a view, or already saved, Ctrl+S replaces that view directly.
  • Views library… (Ctrl+O) lists the views of the observed container, or of every container, with a thumbnail, most recent first or by name. A view always opens in a new tab named after it.
  • A view whose components are no longer all in the container is flagged. It opens without them, after a warning that names them.
  • Views can be renamed, deleted, exported to a file to share them, and imported from one.

Components and Sequences tabs can be renamed: double-click a tab's title, or use Rename in its menu. The title of a components tab is the name proposed when saving it. Renaming a tab opened from a view detaches it from that view: saving it then proposes a new view instead of replacing the old one.

Views are stored in the views folder set in Settings, one sub-folder per container, each view in a .cow file with a .png thumbnail beside it.

Sequences

A sequence diagram records the messages exchanged between components while it runs: service calls and their returns, event notifications, parameter reads and the actions one component triggers on another.

Recording

Recordings are made of the data flows, so the Flows switch must be on. Then, from a component diagram:

  • select one or more components and click Record in the header;
  • or click Record this component in the details panel;
  • or right-click a link and choose to record a sequence between its two components.

Threads records too: see Threads.

The recording opens in a Sequences tab and goes on until you click Stop, or Record again from any panel: one recording runs at a time, whoever started it, and the other ways to start one are grayed meanwhile. The scope shows which components it covers.

Reading a sequence

  • Live drawing draws the messages as they arrive. When off, the diagram stays as it is while the recording goes on, and catches up when turned back on or when the recording stops.
  • Follow live keeps the latest message in view. It turns off when you scroll back or jump to a moment on the timeline.
  • The kind switches show or hide services, events, parameters and actions.
  • Click a message to see its details: its two ends, its interface and method, its thread, its arguments, its result or failure, its wait in the queue for an asynchronous event, and its duration with the durations of the last calls of the same method and their 95th percentile. Show in diagram opens the component diagram where the two components are linked. The details panel folds down to a strip, like the side panels of Components.
  • Save the log, the arrow beside the snapshot in the band, saves every recorded message, filtered or not, as text, CSV (one row per message) or JSON Lines (one object per line); the file's extension picks the format, the one of the Settings is offered first.
  • The folder button, beside it, opens the logs folder. With Write each sequence recording to disk as it goes on in Settings, every message is also written, as it is recorded, to a file of that folder named after the scope and the start time, which the folder button's tooltip names; the file ends when the recording stops. Unlike a log saved at the end, it keeps everything recorded even if the process dies. In JSON Lines, the file can be read at any moment.

Timeline

Under the diagram, the timeline covers the whole recording, cut into time slices. It shows how many messages happened in each slice (the volume), and how long the slowest invocation started in each slice took, on a logarithmic scale (the duration). The span shown in the diagram is outlined. Click or drag on the timeline to bring the diagram to that moment. How volume and duration are drawn together is chosen in Settings.

A duration is measured from the start to the end of an invocation. For an asynchronous event, the time it waited in the delivery queue before its consumer was notified is shown apart, as Waited in queue in the message details.

Dependency graph

The dependency graph shows which component depends on which, through its ports: a component depends on the components whose services it uses, whose events it consumes and whose parameters it accesses. It is computed each time the graph is opened, while the container is started, whatever the container parameters. Circular dependency checking only governs the verification made at start.

  • Component tree, on the left, scopes a large graph; the panel folds down to a strip like the side panels of Components. A click on a composite shows it and all its descendants down to the leaf components, with the dependencies between them; a click on a leaf centers it. The triangle opens or closes a composite without scoping. The breadcrumb above the graph goes back up, to the whole graph. Cycles, statistics and the status bar follow the scope; the status bar counts the dependencies with the outside it hides.
  • Outside neighbors, a toggle in the Structure group, adds to a scoped graph the components outside it that one of its components depends on, or that depend on one of them, toned down, with those dependencies.
  • Composition links join each composite to its children, drawn as UML compositions: a solid line with a filled diamond on the composite's side. A switch in the toolbox shows or hides them.
  • Cycles detected lists the circular dependencies. Each cycle has its own color on its components, its composition links and the dependencies that make it. Select a cycle to highlight its components.
  • Statistics: the number of components, dependencies, cycles and isolated components. A component linked by composition is not isolated. The panel on the right, with the cycles and the statistics, folds down to a strip like the component tree.

How cycles take composition into account

A composite is started only once all of its children are. Depending on a component held by a composite therefore means waiting for that whole composite. A cycle may go through composites: in a step X → Y, either X composes Y, or X depends on Y or on one of its descendants. For example, with A composing A1 and A2, B composing B1 and B2, A1 depending on B2 and B1 depending on A2, the cycle is A → A1 → B → B1 → A: neither A nor B can be started first. A child using a port of its own composite is not a cycle.

Click a component to highlight what it depends on and what depends on it; a click on the background clears the highlight and the selected cycle.

Right-click a component or a link for its menu: highlight, hide, show or hide dependencies, go to the source or the target of a link.

Threads

Threads shows what the observed container's worker pools are doing: the pool delivering asynchronous events (only when events are asynchronous), the async manager's shared general pool, and its dedicated pools, one per queue key. Calls and synchronous events run on the caller's thread, outside these pools: while the data flows are on, the threads of the application that run the components' calls - such as a simulation thread or the JavaFX application thread - show too, under Calling threads; for them, a task is a call of a component, and Covalent's own components' calls are left out. The pools are sampled four times a second; the last quarter of an hour is kept, of which the range selector shows the last 15 s, minute, 5 or 15 minutes.

Task tracking

The JVM tells whether a thread runs, waits or is blocked; only the pool knows whether it is in a task. Task tracking asks the pools to tell which task each worker runs, and how long tasks wait in the queue and run. It is off until you turn it on, in the panel's header or from the cockpit's Threads tile; its cost is too small to measure (see below). It stays on until you turn it off or leave the container; Settings can turn it on when connecting. While it is off, a waiting thread and an idle one cannot be told apart: both show striped, as waits or idle, and occupancy only counts running and blocked threads.

What task tracking and data flows give

With neither option on, Threads shows what a general-purpose JVM tool such as VisualVM shows: the state of each thread over time (running, waiting, blocked on a monitor) and deadlocks. On top of that it knows the container's worker pools: their workers, queues, rejected tasks and contention, and it can resize them live. What it cannot tell is whether a waiting pool thread is idle or holds a task.

Task tracking asks the pools which task each worker runs and how long tasks wait and run. It is what makes Threads really useful, and it is worth turning on whenever you work with it: it tells a waiting thread from an idle one, names the task on each lane, measures occupancy as a share of time rather than as an instant sample, gives waiting and running times and the load per component, and reports long tasks. It is off until you turn it on, in the panel's header or from the cockpit's Threads tile, or with the option of Settings that does it at each connection.

Data flows observe the calls of the whole container. In Threads, they show under Calling threads the application's own threads that run component calls (a simulation thread, the JavaFX thread), with their call rate, report their long calls, and make it possible to record a sequence from a thread.

Costs. Task tracking costs almost nothing: in our tests it made no measurable difference to the time or the garbage of a task, and the memory it keeps is negligible. Data flows cost much more, because every call between components produces instrumentation events that the supervisor then processes. With the supervisor connected, that is about 7 % of one CPU core at 1,000 calls per second, 15 to 30 % at 5,000 and 40 % at 20,000. Each call also creates about 5 kilobytes of short-lived objects that the garbage collector must clean up (about 50 megabytes per second at 10,000 calls per second); the memory they keep permanently is small: about half a megabyte in our test container, and it did not grow with the number of calls. A container at rest is not affected. Turn the flows off when you do not need them, especially on code that makes many calls. Recording a sequence adds to this cost, because the arguments of each call are printed and the messages are kept.

Thread states

  • Runs (green): in a task, using CPU, or waiting on blocking I/O, which the JVM also reports as running. The selected thread's CPU time tells them apart.
  • Waits (amber): in a task, waiting: a sleep, a future, a condition, a java.util.concurrent lock. It holds its worker without using CPU.
  • Blocked (monitor) (red): blocked on a synchronized monitor. The thread holding it is shown.
  • Idle (gray): no task.

For sizing: a pool saturated with green threads is CPU-bound, and more threads do not help once the cores are busy; saturated with amber, it waits on something else, and more workers help, or the wait can be fixed; red is lock contention, a matter of code, not of size.

Pools

Each pool sits in a compartment of its own, its figures in aligned columns: its workers, its queue, its occupancy (the share of busy workers), and while tasks are tracked the 95th percentile of how long tasks waited and ran over the range shown (hover for the median). While tasks are tracked, occupancy counts the time the workers spent in tasks between two samples, however short the tasks, and the pool shows the tasks it completes per second. Its contention badge sums up the last minute, as the tiles do: Saturated when it rejected tasks within the minute - "calmer now" says when its last seconds are quieter - or, over the last five seconds, when nearly all its workers stayed busy while tasks queued most of the time; Under strain when most of them stayed busy, or when tasks queued most of the time; Fluid otherwise. Pools and threads use the same three colors, but they do not say the same thing: a pool can be saturated with green threads only, and a fluid pool can hold a blocked thread.

Tuning a pool live: click the workers or queue figure of the events pool or of the general pool. Workers go one at a time, from 1 to 64; the queue capacity by halves or doubles, from 16 to 65536. Each change applies at once and lasts until the container restarts, its configuration then applying again: the way to try a size before setting it there. A changed figure is outlined, with a dot, and its tooltip and reset give its value when the supervisor connected. The general pool's queue capacity also applies to the dedicated pools. A dedicated pool keeps its single worker, which keeps its tasks in order.

Reading the panel

  • Tiles: the threads, the average and peak occupancy, the queued tasks and the rejections over the range shown.
  • Occupancy: the share of busy workers per kind of pool over time; hover for the values at a moment. With task tracking off, each sample only says whether a worker is busy at that instant, so the curve jumps between 0 and 100 %; on, it gives the share of time spent in tasks.
  • Pool health: one sentence on the most pressed pool, colored by its contention, such as "Async Services pool overwhelmed: 372 tasks rejected in the last minute"; then each pool's occupancy over the minute, its contention and why, on one line - its rejections within the minute, its stuck threads, calmer now; hover it for the whole line - with Tune… to resize it; the recent events; and, folded, Threads now: how many threads are in each state now - a saturated pool's threads all run, so this says what they do, not how pressed the pools are. The recent events are: rejections, a pool saturating then fluid again (one entry each, however it wavers in between), resized pools and changed queue capacities, blocked threads, long tasks, deadlocks.
  • Container threads: one lane per thread, all on the same time axis, so that what happens at the same moment lines up. Hover a lane to read every lane at that moment; under each thread's name, a dot of its state and what it does. A pill at the lane's right end says what it works on: its current task's name while one lasts - an async task submitted without a queue key, which has none, shows the component that submitted it; otherwise the component it worked for most over the last five seconds, with that share of its time, however short its tasks; otherwise, outlined, the last component it worked for and how long ago. Tasks too short to be caught by a sample - typically event deliveries, a few microseconds each - show as a green bar rising from the bottom of an idle lane, as high as the share of the interval they took, and the thread reads short tasks with how busy it was. Group by gathers the lanes by pool, or, while tasks are tracked, shows the load per component: a pool's worker takes any task, so a thread belongs to no component; instead, one row per component - the one that submitted an async task, or the consumer of an event - shows, for each kind of pool it worked in, the share of that pool's workers that worked for it at each moment, however short its tasks: one line per kind, in the occupancy chart's colors, up to the dashed line of 100 % - a component that keeps every worker of a pool busy reaches it. Under its name, its average share of each kind; its tooltip gives the peaks. The rows keep their order while time passes. Hover a row to read its shares at that moment. Tasks of no known component, such as one submitted before tracking was on, go to other tasks.
  • Selected thread: click a lane to see its state, current task, lock, CPU use, its occupancy over five minutes and what it did lately. Its call stack lists the methods of the application's components it is running, innermost first, read from its stack every second; Covalent's own components are left out. Java stack shows every frame.
  • Recording a sequence: Record, in the header, records what the selection gives. With a thread selected, it records the last component the thread worked for. Click its lane at a moment to record instead the components it worked for then; the moment is outlined, and a second click lets it go. A calling thread's components are all known, however short its calls; a pool's worker's are known only when a sample caught its task, with task tracking on.

Pause freezes what is shown while sampling goes on: a slider above the lanes then moves back in time over the last quarter of an hour. Resume comes back to now. Copy report copies the pools and their threads as text, for a bug report. A deadlock among the pools' threads shows as a red banner. Two buttons go with it: Copy deadlock report copies, for each deadlocked thread, its state, the lock it waits for, the locks it holds and its stack; Interrupt threads, after a confirmation, interrupts them. That frees the threads waiting interruptibly, not those blocked on a synchronized monitor, which only a restart of the container frees. A task running longer than the threshold chosen in Settings (10 seconds by default) is reported in the recent events.

Exclusions

Excluded components appear neither in the diagrams nor in the recordings. Excluding a composite excludes everything it contains.

  • The Built-in exclusion is the supervisor's own interface component. It is always excluded and cannot be removed.
  • Add a component by its fully qualified name, then Exclude. Remove it with the bin icon of its row.
  • Exclude internal components hides Covalent's own components, marked @Internal. Turn it off to see them.

The exclusions are kept from one run to the next. A component excluded while observing another application is kept too, and applies again whenever that application is observed.

Stereotypes

A stereotype is the short label written in a component's card and in the sequence diagrams, such as Repository or Composite. A component takes the stereotype matching the end of its name, and the longest matching ending wins. With no match, the supervisor falls back on what the metamodel says: Composite, Component, Raw, Interface or Internal.

The Stereotypes function has no button on the rail: it is the Stereotypes tab of Settings, also opened with Component stereotypes… in the General tab.

  • Built-in stereotypes come with the supervisor and are locked. Tick Allow removing the built-in stereotypes to remove them as well; a removed built-in stereotype stays removed from one run to the next. Restore the initial list brings back the built-in stereotypes and removes the ones you added, as at installation.
  • To add one, give a name ending, made of letters, digits, _ and $, and the stereotype to show, which has a length limit. An ending that already has a stereotype is refused. Remove an added stereotype with the button of its row. The stereotypes you add are kept from one run to the next.
  • The filter field narrows the list.
  • Try a component name shows which stereotype a name gets, which ending won, and which other endings also match.

Settings

Settings has three tabs. General groups the preferences in cards: Data flows, Threads, Components, Language and Diagram. Colors sets the colors of the diagrams. Stereotypes holds the stereotype table.

  • Data flows on when connecting to a container: turns the flows on at each connection.
  • Task tracking on when connecting to a container: turns on the task tracking of Threads at each connection.
  • Report a task running longer than: the threshold of the long task reports of Threads.
  • Exclusion Policy… and Component stereotypes…: open the Exclusions function and the Stereotypes tab.
  • Recalculate diagram layout when hiding components: lays the diagram out again when components are hidden, instead of leaving gaps.
  • New sequence diagrams start with live drawing on.
  • Sequence timeline: volume or duration with a switch, volume bars with a duration line, or duration bars with a volume density band.
  • Write each sequence recording to disk as it goes: keeps a live log of every new recording, which survives a crash; Log format: the format of the sequence logs.
  • Snapshot folder, Views folder and Logs folder: where snapshots, saved views and live sequence logs go.
  • Supervisor language: the system language, English or French, applied at the next start.
  • Colors: the colors of the diagrams, per theme, with a live preview. Hovering a color shows where it is used. Restore default colors brings back the default colors of the current theme, after a confirmation; the other theme keeps its own.

The light and dark themes switch from the header, at any time. All settings are kept from one run to the next.
